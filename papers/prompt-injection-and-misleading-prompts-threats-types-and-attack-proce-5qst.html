Prompt Injection and Misleading Prompts Threats, Types, and Attack Processes in AI system

By Celvien · AI Prompting · 20 November 2026

The paper "Prompt Injection and Misleading Prompts: Threats, Types, and Attack Processes in AI Systems", published on November 4, 2026 by @celvein, examines why prompt injection remains a top risk for large language model (LLM) applications. Because models process developer rules, user requests, retrieved data, and tool outputs inside a single text stream without a hardware-enforced boundary separating commands from untrusted content, they cannot reliably distinguish trusted instructions from untrusted text. Combined with instruction-following training, probabilistic outputs, and autonomous agents with tool access, malicious phrasing can turn directly into unintended actions. Every frontier model evaluated in the cited 2026 studies showed vulnerability, and defenses relying solely on the model struggle against adaptive attackers, pointing toward system-level controls outside the model. To categorize these threats, the paper outlines attack types including direct prompt injection, jailbreaking, indirect prompt injection (hidden in external sources like emails, web pages, or documents), multimodal or cross-modal injection, knowledge or RAG poisoning, tool and protocol injection (such as MCP and A2A), cross-agent injection, and agent data injection. Conceptually, attackers choose a goal and target, map what the AI can read or execute, craft authoritative or concealed payloads, place them where the AI ingests them, wait for execution, collect outcomes, and iterate—sometimes using automated optimization or persistent memory and knowledge stores. The severity of these attacks scales with what the AI is allowed to access and do, threatening confidentiality (leaking prompts, credentials, or data), integrity (corrupting answers or records), availability (deleting data or triggering runaway tool use), financial safety, and user trust. Concealment, low technical barriers, and "confused deputy" permissions make these risks especially difficult to manage. Evidence from 2025–2026 reports and benchmarks—including the Indirect Prompt Injection Arena, Palo Alto Networks Unit 42, Forcepoint, the Cloud Security Alliance, OWASP, LLMail-Inject, AutoDojo, and PIArena—shows concealed attacks succeeding across tested models, attackers adapting to static defenses, and many incidents lacking a CVE. For defense, the paper argues for layered controls rather than relying on a single measure, highlighting least privilege and capability gating, out-of-band deterministic policy enforcement, data and instruction separation, and output or action validation alongside input filters, model hardening, and monitoring or red-teaming. Ordinary users are advised to treat AI summaries of unknown external content cautiously, restrict unnecessary account or payment access, and keep human approval on high-impact actions. Looking ahead, the field faces ongoing challenges around adaptive evaluation, semantic and data manipulation, expanding agent and multimodal surfaces, usability trade-offs, and incomplete incident tracking, driving a shift toward system-level enforcement that assumes the model can be fooled.

Prompt Injection and Misleading Prompts: Threats, Types, and Attack Processes in AI Systems Nov 4, 2026 · @celvein

Abstract

Prompt injection is the top-ranked risk for applications built on large language models (LLMs), because these models cannot reliably tell trusted instructions from untrusted data. This paper explains how prompt injection and other misleading prompts work, classifies the main attack types, and describes the stages attackers follow at a conceptual level. It then reviews why the attacks are dangerous, drawing on 2026 public red-team competitions, in-the-wild detections and defense evaluations. The main finding is that every frontier model tested so far has been shown vulnerable, that defenses relying on the model itself fail against adaptive attackers, and that the strongest protection comes from system design: least privilege, strict separation of data from instructions, and deterministic checks outside the model. The paper is written for education and defense; it deliberately omits ready-to-use attack strings.

1. Introduction

Prompt injection means giving an AI system text that changes its behavior in ways its developer or user did not intend. OWASP lists it as LLM01, the first item in its Top 10 for LLM applications, and its 2026 guidance keeps it at the top while extending the scope to cross-modal injection (images and other media). The problem matters more now than in 2023 because AI is no longer only a chatbot. Assistants read email, browse the web, run code, query databases and call tools. Each of those inputs is a channel through which an attacker can talk to the model without ever talking to the user. This paper answers three questions: i. How do prompt injection and misleading prompts work, and why do they succeed? ii. What types exist, and what process do attackers follow? iii. Why are they dangerous, and what reduces the risk? A note on scope: "misleading prompts" here covers any input crafted to push a model away from its intended behavior, including direct instruction overrides, jailbreaks, hidden instructions in content, and poisoned knowledge sources.

2. Background: why the problem exists

An LLM receives one long stream of text and predicts what comes next. The developer's rules (the system prompt), the user's request, a retrieved web page and a tool result all arrive in that same stream. There is no hardware-enforced boundary between "command" and "content", so a sentence phrased like an instruction can be treated as one wherever it appears. OWASP's own description puts it this way: models cannot inherently distinguish developer instructions from user input, which makes injection a pervasive attack surface (OWASP, via Radar guide). The same guidance notes the parallel with classic injection flaws such as SQL injection, where untrusted input reaches an interpreter unneutralized. The difference is that SQL has a strict grammar that can be escaped; natural language does not. Three properties make the situation worse: #Instruction-following is the product. The model is trained to comply with text, so obedience is the feature being exploited. #Probabilistic behavior. OWASP states that, given the stochastic nature of models, it is unclear whether any fool-proof prevention exists; mitigations only reduce impact. #Growing autonomy. Agents act on results with less human oversight, so a successful injection turns directly into an action rather than a wrong sentence. The two foundational academic papers are Perez and Ribeiro (2022), which studied direct override of a developer's prompt, and Greshake et al. (2023), which showed that instructions hidden in external content can compromise real LLM-integrated applications.

4. How attackers do it: the process

Public incident reports and research describe a fairly consistent life cycle. The stages below stay at the level of purpose and pattern; they are what defenders need to model, not a recipe. i. Choose a goal and a target. The attacker decides what they want (stolen data, a fraudulent action, a bypassed filter, free service, manipulated search or ad results) and which AI system is likely to hold the access needed. ii. Map what the AI reads and can do. For an indirect attack, the key questions are which content sources the assistant ingests (web pages, mail, shared files, tickets, code repositories) and which tools it may call. For a direct attack, it is how the system prompt and filters behave. iii. Craft the payload. Attackers phrase the text so the model treats it as authoritative. Common patterns include imitating a system or developer message, addressing the model directly (for example, opening with a line that speaks to "any AI reading this"), wrapping the request in role-play or fiction, and hiding it from humans while keeping it machine-readable. Unit 42 documented 22 distinct techniques used in real web payloads, and Forcepoint found that many share recognizable override and "if you are an LLM" triggers. iv. Deliver it where the AI will find it. The payload is placed in a web page, an email, a shared document, a calendar invite, a package or repository, an image, or a tool description. The attacker never needs access to the victim's account; they only need the AI to read the content. v. Wait for ingestion and execution. When a user or an automated job asks the AI to summarize, search or process that content, the model reads the payload along with everything else and may act on it, often through a tool call. vi. Collect the outcome. The result might be leaked data, a sent message, a changed record, a purchase, a corrupted answer shown to the user, or a deleted file. Many payloads are written to stay concealed from the user, as the 2026 public competition below measured. vii. Iterate and persist. Attackers test variants until one works. Research now automates this step: automated attack methods adapted to agents use search and optimization against tool-calling systems, and reinforcement-learning attackers train a model to improve injected prompts against a defense. Content stored in an AI's memory or knowledge base can also keep working after the first exposure.

Attacker objectives seen in the wild

The Unit 42 and Forcepoint reports describe goals that include evading AI-based ad review, bypassing content moderation, coercing an AI agent into a paid subscription, financial fraud, API key theft and data destruction. These are ordinary criminal motives applied to a new target: the AI that acts for the user.

8. Open challenges and outlook

Evaluation gaps. Many defenses were validated only on fixed attack sets. Adaptive benchmarks such as AutoDojo and PIArena show those numbers can give a false sense of security, so defenses need testing against attackers who know them. Semantic attacks. When an attack corrupts what the model believes instead of what it is told, filters trained on instruction-like patterns have little to catch. Content verification and provenance tracking become necessary. Wider surfaces. Multimodal inputs, long contexts, agent-to-agent messages and tool protocols all add channels. OWASP's 2026 scope now includes cross-modal injection. Cost of safety. Least privilege and approval prompts reduce risk but also reduce convenience, so products face pressure to loosen them. Measurement. Because injection often receives no CVE, there is no complete public count of incidents, which makes risk hard to size. The likely direction, supported by the 2026 evidence, is a shift from asking the model to police itself toward system-level controls that assume the model can be fooled.

9. Conclusion

Prompt injection works because language models read instructions and data as one stream of text. That single design fact produces direct attacks by users, indirect attacks by outsiders, jailbreaks, multimodal and tool-based variants, and poisoned knowledge sources. The harm grows with the AI's access: leaked data, fraud, destroyed files and manipulated answers are all documented in 2026 reports. Model-level defenses and filters help against casual attempts but have repeatedly failed against adaptive attackers. The most reliable protection so far comes from limiting what the AI can do, treating all external content as untrusted, and enforcing rules outside the model. Developers should design for the assumption that injection will sometimes succeed, and users should keep humans in the loop for any action that matters.
