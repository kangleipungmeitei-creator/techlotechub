Meta / Instagram Security Architecture & Account Recovery Explained

By Sorokhaibam Nungsiba Singh · Meta · 20 November 2026

This technical report explains how Meta’s identity and access management (IAM) system protects billions of users across its platforms using dynamic trust scaling, three authentication layers (salted password hashing with bcrypt/Argon2, offline TOTP 2FA, and device fingerprinting/velocity checks), and backend components including TLS 1.3, edge servers, API gateways, TAO, Memcached, and the Risk Engine. It also covers account recovery across full credentials, forgotten passwords, and zero-access cases (via session history, liveness-checked video selfies, or government ID verification), alongside a practical Instagram recovery guide, graduated verification, human review, backend controls summary, and a glossary.

Meta / Instagram Security Architecture & Account Recovery Explained

Preface: A Note to the Reader

Imagine you are sitting in a classroom, and the topic of the day is something you interact with every single day without thinking about it - logging into Instagram, or recovering a Facebook account you lost access to. You tap a button, type a password, maybe enter a six-digit code that appeared on your phone, and you are in. Simple, right?

But behind that single tap, something extraordinary is happening. Hundreds of servers spread across multiple countries are making split-second decisions about who you are, whether you should be trusted, and whether the person trying to get in is really you or someone pretending to be you. This report is your guided tour through all of that.

Think of this document the way you would think of a knowledgeable friend who works as an engineer at a major tech company and is willing to sit down with you and explain everything not in jargon-filled technical language, but in plain terms that build your understanding layer by layer. By the time you finish reading, you will understand not just how Meta keeps accounts safe, but why every design decision was made the way it was.

This document is structured so that each section builds on the previous one. If you are new to the topic, it is recommended to read from the beginning. Each major concept is introduced with an analogy before the technical detail is explained. Technical terms are defined when first introduced, and a summary table is included at the end for quick reference.

Section 1: What Is Identity and Access Management?

Before we look at Instagram specifically, we need to understand a foundational concept in computer security: Identity and Access Management, commonly abbreviated as IAM.

Here is the simplest way to think about it. Imagine a building with thousands of rooms. Each room contains something valuable - private messages, financial data, personal photos. The building has a front door, a receptionist, and a complex key system. IAM is the entire system that decides who is allowed through the front door, which rooms they can enter, and what they can do once they are inside.

Meta's IAM system operates at a scale that is almost impossible to fully comprehend. The company serves more than three billion users across Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp, and Messenger. Every single login attempt, every password reset, every account recovery request billions of them every single day - passes through this system.

The fundamental question that IAM must answer is deceptively simple: Is the person trying to get in really who they claim to be? This is called authentication. But authentication alone is not enough. Once someone is inside, the system must also ask: Is this person allowed to do what they are trying to do? This is called authorization.

You can think of authentication as showing your passport at the airport, and authorization as your boarding pass, which confirms you are allowed on a specific flight. Both steps are necessary, and Meta's system handles both with extraordinary sophistication.

One of the most important concepts in Meta's IAM design is something called dynamic trust scaling. This means that the level of verification required from you is not fixed it adjusts based on how much risk the system detects.

If you are logging in from your own phone, on your home Wi-Fi, at the usual time of day, the system recognises all of these signals and lets you in with minimal friction. But if you are trying to log in from a computer you have never used before, in a different country, at three in the morning, the system rightly becomes suspicious and requires you to prove your identity with much stronger evidence.

This is not just good security design - it is also good user experience design, because it means that ordinary users going about their normal lives are not constantly burdened with extra verification steps. The extra scrutiny is reserved for situations that genuinely warrant it.

Section 2: The Three Layers of Security

Meta's authentication model is built on three distinct layers, each one adding a degree of certainty that the person logging in is genuine. Security professionals sometimes refer to these three layers using the shorthand: something you know, something you have, and something you are. Let us walk through each one carefully.

The first and most familiar layer is your password, representing something you know. When you type your password and press log in, it feels like a simple act, but a great deal happens on Meta's servers in the moments that follow.

The first thing to understand is that Meta does not store your password. Let that sink in for a moment. The company that runs the platform you log into every day has absolutely no record of what your password actually is. What they store instead is something called a hash.

Here is an analogy to make this clear. Imagine you take the word 'sunshine' and put it through a special machine that converts it into the string 'a7f8d2e1.' The machine works in only one direction you can always put 'sunshine' in and get 'a7f8d2e1' out, but you can never put 'a7f8d2e1' in and get 'sunshine' back. This one-way transformation is what a cryptographic hash function does. Meta stores only the transformed version.

When you log in, the system takes whatever password you typed, runs it through the same transformation, and compares the result to what is stored. If they match, you are in. If a hacker ever stole Meta's database, they would find nothing but a long list of scrambled strings that are computationally impossible to reverse.

The specific algorithms Meta uses for this - bcrypt and Argon2 - are deliberately designed to be slow and computationally expensive. This is intentional. It means that even if someone tried to guess passwords by running millions of guesses through the same transformation, it would take an impractically long time.

Passwords are also salted, meaning a random string of characters is added to your password before hashing it. This ensures that even if two users have the same password, their stored hashes are completely different, making large-scale attacks far less effective.

Even a perfectly hashed password has a weakness: if someone discovers your password perhaps you used it on another website that got hacked, or you fell for a phishing email - they now have everything they need to get in. This is why the second layer exists.

Two-Factor Authentication, commonly called 2FA, represents something you have and requires you to prove not just that you know the password, but that you physically possess a specific device. The most secure form of 2FA uses an authenticator app like Google Authenticator or Duo. Understanding how this works reveals something genuinely elegant about modern cryptography.

TOTP stands for Time-based One-Time Password. Here is the remarkable part: the code your phone generates is calculated entirely offline. Your phone does not communicate with Meta's servers to get the code. It creates the code independently, and yet Meta's servers know what it will be.

This is possible because of a shared secret. When you first set up 2FA on Instagram, the app shows you a QR code. Hidden inside that QR code is a long random string of characters - the shared secret. Your phone saves this secret. Meta's server also saves this secret.

Every thirty seconds, your phone's authenticator app takes the current time (expressed as a large number called a Unix timestamp), mathematically combines it with the shared secret using a process called HMAC-SHA1, and produces a six-digit code. Meta's server does the exact same calculation at the exact same time. Because both sides start with the same secret and the same time, they arrive at the same six-digit number. When you type that code into Instagram, the server checks whether what you entered matches what it calculated. If it does, you are authenticated.

Because clocks can drift slightly, Meta actually calculates three codes simultaneously: the one for the current thirty-second window, the one for the previous window, and the one for the next window. If your code matches any of them, you get in. This tolerance window is small enough to keep things secure but large enough to account for minor clock discrepancies.

The third layer - something you are, or device fingerprinting - operates silently in the background. You do not see it and you do not interact with it directly, but it is arguably the most powerful and sophisticated of the three.

While your password and 2FA code are being verified, Meta's Risk Engine is simultaneously running an analysis of who you appear to be based on dozens of environmental signals. This analysis happens in milliseconds and considers factors including your IP address and the location it maps to, the type of device you are using, the browser version and its configuration, something called a canvas fingerprint a unique rendering signature produced by your specific combination of graphics hardware and software the language settings of your browser, the time of day, and your account's historical login patterns.

The system builds a profile of what a normal login looks like for your specific account. When a new login attempt arrives, it is compared against this profile. A login from your iPhone on your home broadband connection on a Tuesday evening is flagged as low risk. A login from a Windows PC in a country you have never visited, arriving at two in the morning, is flagged as high risk.

One of the most powerful signals the system watches for is called geographic velocity. If you logged in from Tokyo at noon and a second login attempt arrives from Berlin at 12:10 pm, the system knows that is physically impossible. No human being can travel that distance in ten minutes. This impossible travel pattern triggers an immediate block and sends you a Suspicious Login Alert.

Section 3: The Three Recovery Scenarios

Now that we understand how Meta verifies identity under normal circumstances, we can look at what happens when things go wrong. Meta's recovery system is designed around three distinct scenarios, each representing a different level of difficulty and risk.

Scenario One is when you have all your credentials. This is the most common and the simplest case. You know your password and you have access to your 2FA device. The system's job here is straightforward: verify the password, verify the 2FA code, check the device fingerprint, and if everything checks out, issue a session token and let you in.

A session token is essentially a digital pass that your browser or app stores. It says, in effect, 'this person has already authenticated; you do not need to check again for the next several hours (or until they log out).' This is why you do not need to re-enter your password every single time you open Instagram.

Scenario Two is when you have forgotten your password. This is where the concept of out-of-band verification becomes important. Out-of-band means using a completely separate channel to verify your identity one that a hacker who has compromised your password would not automatically also control.

The standard process works as follows: you enter your username or email address, and Meta generates a secure, single-use token a login link or a six-digit code - which it sends to your registered email address or phone number. This token has a very short expiry window, typically between ten and fifteen minutes. If you can receive this code, you can prove that you control the account's associated contact details, and Meta allows you to set a new password.

There is an additional shortcut available called the trusted device fast-path. If you are using a device or browser that has a long and consistent history of successful logins to your account, Meta's risk engine recognises the hardware fingerprint and may allow you to bypass the email or SMS step entirely, letting you reset your password directly through an in-app prompt. This is a concession to convenience for legitimate users, carefully balanced against the very low risk that someone who has both your forgotten password and your trusted personal device is actually an attacker.

Scenario Three is when you have lost everything. This is the most challenging scenario, and the one that the majority of this report's technical depth is devoted to. You have lost your password, you no longer have access to the email address linked to your account, and you do not have the phone number associated with it either. This is called zero-access recovery.

When this happens, the standard cryptographic trust chain is completely broken. There is no mathematical proof available to the system that you are who you claim to be. Instead, Meta must switch from automated digital checks to human identity verification and behavioural analysis.

Think of it this way: the system cannot check your password because you do not have it. It cannot send a code to your email because you cannot access it. It cannot send an SMS because you do not have that number. So it needs to find another way to answer the question: Is this the actual person who owns this account?

In Stage One, environmental verification, before Meta even shows you the advanced recovery options, it checks where the request is coming from. The backend system queries the account's historical session logs a record of every device and network that has successfully logged into the account over the past ninety days.

If your recovery request is coming from a device and network that appear in that history, the system sees this as a promising sign and allows you to proceed. If the request is coming from an entirely unrecognised device on an unfamiliar network, the system issues what is called a hard block. The reasoning is simple: a legitimate account owner trying to recover their account is much more likely to be doing so from their own home or workplace than from an anonymous location. A remote hacker, by contrast, is almost certain to be connecting from a device and location that has no history with the account.

In Stage Two, video selfie verification, if your account contains photos of your face from profile pictures or tagged posts Meta will ask you to take a video selfie. This is not simply recording a short clip of yourself. It is a sophisticated computer vision process designed to answer two separate questions at once.

The first question is: Is this a real, live human being, or is someone holding up a photograph or playing a video on another screen? This is called a liveness check. The app will ask you to turn your head left, right, up, and down. As you do this, Meta's servers analyse the video frame by frame, looking for depth data that a flat photograph cannot produce, natural lighting variations that change as a face rotates, reflections in the eyes, and the micro-tremors of natural human movement. A static photo held up to the camera will fail these checks.

The second question is: Does this live face match the face associated with the account? To answer this, the AI model processes your video and extracts what is called a facial embedding a mathematical representation of the geometric relationships between key features of your face. The distances between your eyes, the shape of your nose, the angle of your jawline, the proportions of your forehead - all of these are translated into a string of numbers.

The system then retrieves the profile photos and tagged images from your account's history, runs those through the same embedding model, and calculates the mathematical distance between your live facial embedding and the historical ones. If the similarity score exceeds a strict confidence threshold - estimated to be in the region of 98% the system concludes that the person in the video is the same person who has appeared in the account's photos over time.

If the match is successful, Meta generates a secure account recovery token, flags the old email as unverified, binds your new email to the account, and sends you a reset link.

In Stage Three, government ID submission, for accounts that do not have enough face photos for biometric comparison - including many business accounts or in cases where the video selfie check cannot be completed, Meta requires the submission of a government-issued identity document, such as a passport or driving licence.

The document is processed by automated document analysis models that check for security watermarks, examine font consistency, look for signs of digital tampering, and verify that the physical characteristics of the document match the expected specifications for that document type. The system then checks whether the full legal name and date of birth on the document match the metadata stored in Meta's backend systems for that account.

On privacy during recovery, a question that naturally arises is: does Meta store the facial embeddings and document scans it collects during recovery? The answer is no, and this is not merely a policy choice - it is engineered into the process. Once the mathematical comparison is completed, whether the result is a successful match or a failure, Meta's backend automatically deletes the created facial embeddings and any associated biometric data. This data purge is immediate and applies universally, complying with biometric data privacy laws in multiple jurisdictions.

Section 4: The Backend Infrastructure

We have discussed what happens from your perspective. Now let us look at what is happening inside Meta's systems when you click Log In. What follows is a look at the machinery running underneath the experience.

During the request journey, when you tap Log In, your device sends an encrypted message across the internet. This message uses a protocol called TLS 1.3 - Transport Layer Security. You can think of TLS as a sealed envelope: even if someone intercepts the message while it is in transit, they cannot read what is inside.

That message does not travel directly to a single computer at Meta's headquarters. Instead, it first arrives at an Edge Server one of hundreds of servers that Meta maintains physically close to users in cities around the world. These edge servers exist to reduce the time it takes for your request to travel, because data moves at the speed of light and every millisecond matters. They also act as a first line of defence, filtering out automated attacks before the request proceeds further.

From the edge server, your request passes through an API Gateway - a routing layer that acts like a sophisticated air traffic controller, directing your specific request to whichever backend service is responsible for handling it. A login request gets sent to the Authentication Microservice. A photo upload goes elsewhere. Every type of request has its designated destination.

For the database layer, Meta stores its user data across thousands of database servers. Two technologies are central to how this data is accessed quickly: TAO and Memcached.

TAO, which stands for The Associations and Objects, is Meta's proprietary distributed graph database. A graph database is particularly well suited to social networks because it stores data in terms of objects and the relationships between them a user is an object, a friendship is a relationship, a post is an object, a like is a relationship. TAO allows Meta to navigate these complex webs of connections with extraordinary speed.

Memcached is a caching layer. Rather than looking up frequently accessed data from the main database every single time it is needed, Memcached keeps copies of the most recently and commonly used data in very fast memory. When the Authentication Microservice needs to check your account's password hash, it can retrieve it from Memcached in microseconds rather than waiting for a full database query.

Running in parallel with the Authentication Microservice is the Risk Engine, Meta's AI security guard. This is a machine learning system that processes an enormous number of signals simultaneously for every single login attempt, regardless of how legitimate it appears.

The Risk Engine aggregates what security engineers call session telemetry - a comprehensive picture of the context surrounding a login attempt. This includes the precise browser fingerprint, which is a combination of details about the browser version, installed plugins, screen resolution, and dozens of other technical attributes that together form a profile as distinctive as a fingerprint. It includes canvas rendering data, which is the unique way your specific graphics hardware renders a complex image. It includes the exact IP address and the reputation of that IP address whether it has been associated with spam, botnets, or suspicious activity.

All of these signals are fed into a model that assigns a risk score to the login attempt. If the score is below a certain threshold, the login proceeds without additional friction. If the score exceeds that threshold, additional verification is triggered. If the score is extremely high indicating, for example, an impossible travel pattern the login is blocked outright and the account owner is notified.

For session token generation, when a login attempt passes all the required checks, the backend generates a session token. This is a cryptographically signed string like a wax seal on a letter, it can be verified as authentic without revealing the key used to create it.

This token is stored in your browser or app and sent with every subsequent request. It tells Meta's servers, 'I am the user who successfully authenticated X hours ago; you do not need to ask again.' The token has an expiry time and is invalidated when you log out, helping to limit the damage if a token is ever stolen.

Section 5: The Instagram Account Recovery Guide

This section translates the technical concepts above into a practical, step-by-step guide for recovering an Instagram account when you have lost your phone and no longer have access to your linked email or phone number. This is the zero-access recovery scenario described in Section 3.

In Step One, starting the recovery process, open instagram.com in any web browser, or open the Instagram mobile app on any device. On the login screen, tap or click 'Forgot password?' this link is found directly below the password input field.

On the following screen, you will be asked to identify your account. Enter your Instagram username. If you do not remember your username, ask a friend or family member who follows you on Instagram to check your profile and send it to you. Alternatively, if you remember which email address or phone number you originally used when creating the account, you can enter either of those instead.

In Step Two, indicating that you have no access to linked contacts, after you enter your username, Instagram will normally attempt to send a reset code to your linked email or phone number. Because you do not have access to either of these, you need to look for an alternative option that may appear on this screen.

Depending on your version of the app or website, this option may be labelled 'Can't reset your password?', 'No longer have access to these?', or 'Try another way'. If you see the option 'My account was hacked', this is also an appropriate path to take, as it opens the identity verification support flow designed for exactly this type of situation.

As an important note, only use the 'My account was hacked' option if you genuinely cannot access your linked email or phone number. Using this route when the standard recovery would work may slow down the process and create unnecessary support tickets.

In Step Three, submitting your support request, Instagram's identity verification support flow will ask you to provide information that allows them to verify your claim to the account. You will need to provide the new email address you want to link to the account once access is restored. You will also need to provide a clear explanation of your situation - that you have lost your phone and no longer have access to the email or phone number previously associated with the account. Additionally, provide the type of device you most commonly used to access Instagram in the past for example, iPhone, Samsung Galaxy, iPad, along with the approximate date you created the account, where even a rough estimate such as 'around 2019' is helpful.

Depending on whether your account contains identifiable photos of your face, Instagram will then initiate one of two verification processes.

Under Option A, video selfie verification, if your account has profile photos or tagged photos that show your face clearly, Instagram will ask you to complete a video selfie. Open the Instagram app - this step must be done through the app rather than a web browser. Follow the on-screen prompts to record a short video in which you turn your head in the directions indicated.

The AI system will check that you are a live person and not a photograph or a recording, then compare your face mathematically against the photos on your account. Ensure you are in good lighting, facing the camera directly, and that your face is not partially obscured. Glasses, hats, or heavy makeup are unlikely to cause issues unless they substantially alter your facial geometry.

Under Option B, government ID verification, if your account does not have enough face photos for biometric comparison, or if the video selfie option is not offered to you, you will be asked to upload a government-issued identity document. Acceptable documents typically include a passport, a national identity card, or a driving licence.

Take a clear, well-lit photograph of the document showing all four corners. Ensure the text is fully legible and that there are no reflections or shadows obscuring any part of the document. Meta's automated systems will check the document's security features, compare the name and date of birth against the account's metadata, and process your submission.

Once your support request is submitted, Instagram typically responds within twenty-four to forty-eight hours. The response will be sent to the new email address you provided. Be sure to check both your inbox and your spam or junk folder.

In Step Four, securing your account after recovery, once you regain access to your account, it is essential to immediately take steps to secure it so that this situation does not arise again. First, go to your profile, tap the menu icon (three horizontal lines in the top right corner), and navigate to Settings and Privacy. Second, from there, go to Accounts Center, then Personal Details, then Contact Info, and add your new email address as the primary contact for the account. Third, change your password immediately using a strong, unique password that you have not used on any other website or service, where a password of at least twelve characters mixing upper and lower case letters, numbers, and symbols is recommended. Fourth, enable Two-Factor Authentication by navigating to Settings and Privacy, then Accounts Center, then Password and Security, and finally Two-Factor Authentication, choosing an authenticator app rather than SMS since authenticator apps are more secure. Fifth, consider adding a backup phone number to the account, and download a set of recovery codes from the Two-Factor Authentication settings, storing these recovery codes somewhere safe such as printed on paper kept in a secure location.

Section 6: The Fundamental Security Trade-Off

No discussion of account security would be complete without addressing the core tension that shapes every design decision in this area. Security engineers sometimes refer to it as the usability-security trade-off, and it is particularly acute in account recovery systems.

Looking at the two extremes, imagine two hypothetical versions of Instagram's recovery system, each taken to its extreme. In the first version, account recovery is made as simple as possible. You enter a username, explain that you have lost access, and the system automatically updates your linked email and sends you a reset link. This system would never leave a legitimate user locked out of their account. But it would also be trivially exploitable. Any attacker who knew your username which is publicly visible on your profile could use this process to steal your account. The ease of recovery would become a catastrophic security vulnerability.

In the second version, account recovery requires a perfect match of every piece of information ever associated with the account. You would need to provide the exact original 2FA secret key, the password you created the account with, the exact GPS coordinates of your most common login location, and a notarised copy of your identity documents verified by a government official. This system would be essentially impenetrable by attackers. But it would also permanently lock out the vast majority of legitimate users who lose their phones, change their email addresses, or simply forget credentials over time.

For Meta's solution, graduated verification, Meta's real system sits between these extremes, but it does not simply split the difference. Instead, it implements graduated verification - the depth of verification required scales with the risk level of the request.

Low-risk requests such as a password reset from a trusted device with access to the linked email require minimal verification. High-risk requests such as a zero-access recovery from an unrecognised device - require biometric or documentary evidence. And extreme-risk requests - such as the same zero-access recovery coming from an IP address in a country the account has never been accessed from before - result in an outright block.

This graduated approach means that the security burden falls primarily on the situations that genuinely require it, rather than being uniformly high (which would frustrate legitimate users) or uniformly low (which would welcome attackers).

Regarding the role of automation and human review, it is worth noting that not all of Meta's recovery decisions are made by automated systems. For particularly complex or ambiguous cases situations where the automated signals are inconclusive Meta employs human review teams who examine the evidence and make a judgment call. This human backstop exists precisely because automated systems, however sophisticated, can make mistakes, and the consequences of incorrectly locking a legitimate user out of their account are significant.

The presence of human reviewers also serves as a deterrent against social engineering attempts by attackers to manipulate customer service staff into granting access. Meta's training and processes for these teams are designed to be resistant to manipulation, though no system is entirely immune.

Section 7: Summary Reference: Backend Security Controls

The following notes provide a consolidated reference for the four core backend security components described throughout this report as a quick-reference summary rather than a replacement for the detailed explanations in the preceding sections.

Bcrypt / Argon2 Hashing converts passwords into an irreversible scrambled string before storing them, helping prevent data breach exposure so that even if the database is stolen, passwords cannot be read, using a salted hash that is computationally expensive to reverse.

TOTP Validation Engine generates and verifies time-based 6-digit codes using a shared secret and the current timestamp, helping prevent credential stuffing so a stolen password alone is useless without the device, with codes valid only for a 30-second window and never transmitted to Meta's servers.

Risk & Velocity Engine continuously monitors login signals including IP address, device model, geography, and speed of travel, helping prevent remote session hijacking and automated brute-force attacks through impossible-travel detection where anomalous sessions are flagged and blocked in real time.

Neural Face Embedding Matcher converts a live video selfie into a mathematical representation and compares it against photos on the account, helping prevent fraudulent account takeovers via social engineering or impersonation, with embeddings matched at a 98% confidence threshold and all biometric data deleted immediately after the check.

Section 8: Glossary of Key Terms

API Gateway is a routing layer that receives incoming requests and directs them to the appropriate backend microservice, acting as the entry point into Meta's internal systems.

Authentication is the process of verifying that a person is who they claim to be, distinguished from authorisation, which determines what an authenticated person is allowed to do.

Bcrypt / Argon2 are cryptographic hash functions specifically designed for password storage, intentionally slow to compute so brute-force attacks are impractical.

Canvas Fingerprint is a unique rendering signature produced by specific combinations of graphics hardware and browser software, used as part of device identification.

Cryptographic Hash is a mathematical function that converts input data into a fixed-length output through a one-way process where the output cannot be used to recover the original input.

DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service) is a type of attack in which massive numbers of requests are sent to a server simultaneously with the goal of overwhelming and disabling it.

Edge Server is a server located physically close to end users, used to reduce latency and filter out attacks before requests reach central data centres.

Facial Embedding is a mathematical representation of the geometric relationships between facial features, used for biometric comparison, rather than a photograph, as a string of numbers derived from one.

IAM (Identity and Access Management) is the overarching system that governs who is allowed to access what resources, under what conditions.

Liveness Check is a verification process that confirms the subject of a video or photo is a real, live person rather than a photograph, recording, or digital manipulation.

Memcached is an in-memory caching system used to store frequently accessed data for rapid retrieval without a full database query.

OOB (Out-of-Band) Verification is using a separate communication channel - such as email or SMS - to verify identity, distinct from the primary authentication channel.

Risk Engine is an AI-powered system that continuously evaluates the risk level of login and recovery attempts based on environmental signals and historical patterns.

Salt (cryptographic) is a random string of characters added to a password before hashing, ensuring that identical passwords produce different hash values.

Session Token is a cryptographically signed credential issued after successful authentication, stored by the client and submitted with subsequent requests to avoid re-authentication.

TAO is Meta's proprietary distributed graph database, designed to store and traverse the complex network of objects and relationships that make up a social network.

TLS 1.3 (Transport Layer Security) is the current standard protocol for encrypting data in transit between a user's device and a server.

TOTP (Time-based One-Time Password) is a short-lived authentication code generated by combining a shared secret with the current time, used in two-factor authentication.

Velocity Check is a security measure that detects physically impossible patterns of movement, such as login attempts from geographically distant locations within an impossibly short time.

Zero-Access Recovery is an account recovery scenario in which the user cannot provide any of the standard authentication credentials - password, email, or phone number.
